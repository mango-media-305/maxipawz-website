The first week with an adopted dog is easier to organize when the goal is clear: make everyday life predictable while you learn about each other. A welcome party, a crowded outing and a basket of unfamiliar toys can wait. Start with a manageable home setup and a plan everyone in the household can follow.

## Prepare one calm starting point

ASPCA recommends a quiet space and securing household hazards before arrival. Choose an area with water, a comfortable resting surface and room to move away from activity. Check doors, windows, cords, medicines and accessible plants before the dog explores.

Make a simple household map: where the dog rests, where meals happen, which door leads outside and which areas are initially unavailable. Our [home comfort guide](/pet-guides/comfort-at-home) can help you arrange that space. A crate is an option only when it is appropriate for the individual dog; it is not a place to force a frightened dog to stay.

## Collect information before the handoff

Ask the shelter or foster carer for the current food and portion, medication instructions, veterinary records and any known handling or escape concerns. Get their preferred follow-up contact. Keep the dog's familiar food available unless the veterinary team directs otherwise.

Write down who will handle breakfast, bathroom trips and evening care. “Someone will do it” can become two dinners or a missed outing. Use one shared record so each person can see what has already happened.

## Let introductions stay small

Humane World for Animals advises keeping early experiences quiet and consistent. Give your dog time to investigate without requiring greetings with every visitor. Arrange introductions to resident animals with guidance from the adoption organization; do not make the first evening a test of sharing toys or food.

Offer contact rather than pursuing it. A dog that moves away needs space. Keep children supervised, and protect the resting area from interruption. Familiar bedding, when available and safe for the dog, can help make a new place less unfamiliar.

## Use a daily observation note

A useful note is descriptive, not a verdict on personality. Try these headings:

- Food offered and eaten, with the time.
- Water access and any obvious change in drinking.
- Bathroom trips and accidents.
- Where the dog chose to rest.
- Situations the dog approached or avoided.
- Questions for the adoption team or veterinarian.

For example, “ate breakfast after the room became quiet” gives a carer more useful information than “stubborn eater.” The record can reveal which parts of the routine need simplifying without assuming that every behavior is permanent.

## Check identification and arrange follow-up

Review the [microchip and identification checklist](/pet-guides/dog-microchip-id-emergency-preparedness), including registration details and a current contact number. Agree on a door-opening routine before deliveries or visitors arrive.

Arrange veterinary follow-up and bring the adoption records. Do not assume that vomiting, diarrhea, pain or reduced appetite is merely adjustment; ask the veterinary team for advice when health changes occur. Seek urgent care for breathing difficulty, collapse or other severe signs.

## Let the dog set the pace of progress

Adjustment timelines differ. The familiar “3-3-3” idea can remind people to be patient, but it is not a deadline or a guarantee. Review the routine at the end of the week: what helped the dog eat, rest, toilet and interact comfortably?

Keep what worked, simplify what did not, and add new experiences gradually. When daily absences become necessary, use our [routine-change guide](/pet-guides/back-to-school-dog-routine) and seek qualified help for distress. A calm, repeatable week is a useful beginning, even when it looks uneventful.
