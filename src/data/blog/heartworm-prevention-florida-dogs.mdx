Florida dog owners already think about mosquitoes because they are part of warm-weather life. For heartworm prevention, though, the useful mindset is even simpler: **do not try to guess when mosquito exposure starts or stops. Build prevention and testing into the dog's normal year-round veterinary routine.**

The American Heartworm Society recommends year-round heartworm prevention for dogs and notes that heartworm disease has been diagnosed in every U.S. state. Its 2025 incidence survey, released in 2026, continues to show the Gulf Coast and Southeast as major heartworm regions. For Miami and South Florida households, that makes consistency much more useful than a seasonal on-and-off approach.

## Heartworm is a mosquito-transmitted parasite problem

Heartworm disease is caused by the parasite *Dirofilaria immitis*. Mosquitoes are essential to transmission: a mosquito feeds on an infected animal, later carries an infective stage of the parasite, and can transmit it when feeding on another susceptible animal.

That means a dog does not need to visit a swamp, live on a farm, or spend all day outdoors to have potential exposure. A mosquito can reach a dog during an ordinary yard break, neighborhood walk, patio visit, or other routine activity.

The American Heartworm Society emphasizes this point in its owner guidance: any part of the country with mosquitoes can have heartworm disease. Florida's climate and mosquito activity make prevention particularly relevant, but the underlying routine is useful nationwide.

## Make prevention a calendar habit, not a weather decision

Heartworm preventives come in different veterinarian-directed forms and schedules. The right product depends on the individual dog, including age, weight, health history, other medications, parasite risks, and how reliably the household can follow the schedule.

Rather than choosing a product from a generic list online, work with your veterinarian to select an FDA-approved option that fits your dog and then follow the prescribed timing exactly. The American Heartworm Society recommends year-round administration of approved heartworm preventives.

The reason consistency matters is easy to misunderstand. Preventives do not create a permanent mosquito-proof shield. They work against susceptible stages of heartworm infection acquired during a preceding period. Delaying doses creates avoidable gaps in that routine.

A practical household system can be simple:

- connect the dose to a recurring calendar reminder;
- keep the next dose or appointment date where more than one adult in the household can see it;
- record when the preventive was actually given rather than relying on memory;
- refill before the supply runs out;
- include the preventive in travel and emergency planning.

For dogs receiving a veterinarian-administered longer-duration product, put the next appointment on the calendar before leaving the clinic.

## Annual testing still matters when prevention is consistent

Prevention and testing do different jobs.

The American Heartworm Society recommends annual heartworm testing for dogs. Testing helps identify infection even when a dog appears healthy and even when the household believes prevention has been given consistently.

That may seem redundant, but real life creates room for mistakes: a dose can be late, medication can be spit out or vomited, a topical product can be applied incorrectly, refill timing can slip, or a dog's prevention history can be incomplete after adoption or rehoming.

Heartworm disease may not produce obvious early signs. Waiting until a dog develops a persistent cough, exercise intolerance, fatigue, weight loss, or other concerning changes is not a substitute for routine testing. If your dog develops symptoms or you have concerns about exposure or prevention history, contact your veterinarian rather than trying to interpret the risk from symptoms alone.

The CDC likewise recommends routine veterinary care and discussing preventive treatment for heartworms and other parasites with your dog's veterinarian.

## If a dose was missed, do not improvise the recovery plan

A missed heartworm preventive dose is the moment when many owners start calculating dates and searching for a universal answer. The safer move is to contact the veterinary team that manages the dog's prevention.

What happens next can depend on the product, how late the dose is, the dog's age, previous testing, and whether there were earlier gaps. The American Heartworm Society advises consulting a veterinarian about restarting prevention and follow-up testing after lapses.

Do not double a dose unless the veterinarian or the product's approved instructions specifically direct that action. Do not switch products simply to "catch up" without professional guidance. And do not assume that a dog looks healthy enough for the gap to be irrelevant.

Instead, have three pieces of information ready when you call:

1. the exact preventive product and strength;
2. the date the last confirmed dose was given or administered;
3. the date of the dog's most recent heartworm test, if known.

That turns a vague "I think we missed one" conversation into information the veterinary team can actually use.

## Mosquito control supports prevention but does not replace it

Reducing mosquito breeding around the home is worthwhile for the whole household. It should be treated as an additional layer, not an alternative to veterinarian-prescribed heartworm prevention.

South Florida yards can collect small amounts of water in places that are easy to overlook after rain or irrigation. In June 2026, the Village of Pinecrest reminded residents to empty or refresh standing water in items such as birdbaths, pet bowls, planters, buckets, and other containers and to check yards after rainfall.

For a dog household, useful habits include:

- emptying water that collects in outdoor containers;
- refreshing outdoor pet water frequently and keeping bowls clean;
- clearing gutters and other places where water remains trapped;
- turning over unused buckets, toys, or equipment that can hold rain;
- keeping screens and doors in good repair where practical;
- following local mosquito-control guidance when mosquito activity is high.

Be careful with insecticides and repellents. A product intended for people, yards, or household surfaces is not automatically safe to apply to a dog. Use veterinary parasite products only as directed for the animal, and follow label instructions for any yard or household mosquito-control product.

## Do not stop outdoor life to avoid mosquitoes

Heartworm prevention should make normal dog life easier to manage, not make every walk feel dangerous.

Dogs still need appropriate bathroom breaks, exercise, sniffing, enrichment, and time outdoors. In Miami, the bigger practical task is coordinating several routine risks at once: heat, hydration, mosquitoes, fleas and ticks, wet-weather conditions, and safe walking times.

Our [Miami dog hydration guide](/pet-guides/dog-hydration-miami-heat) can help with the heat-and-water side of that routine. The heartworm layer is mostly administrative: keep prevention current, keep testing current, and reduce unnecessary mosquito breeding around the home.

Trying to eliminate every mosquito encounter is unrealistic. Maintaining a reliable prevention plan is much more controllable.

## Keep prevention information in your emergency records

Florida households already have good reasons to maintain accessible veterinary records during hurricane season and travel. Add heartworm information to that system.

Keep a record of:

- the preventive product your dog currently uses;
- dose or administration dates;
- the veterinary clinic that prescribed or administers it;
- the most recent heartworm test date and result;
- any known medication reactions or relevant medical history.

The CDC includes a dog's most recent heartworm test result and a supply of heartworm preventive in its pet disaster-kit guidance. That matters because evacuations, boarding, travel, pharmacy closures, and disrupted routines can make an otherwise simple monthly task easier to miss.

## Prevention is much easier than treating established disease

Heartworm disease can damage the heart, lungs, and associated blood vessels. Treatment of established infection requires professional veterinary management and is not equivalent to simply restarting routine prevention.

That is why the most useful owner strategy happens before there is a problem: prevent infection consistently, test on the recommended schedule, and address lapses promptly with the veterinary team.

If a dog tests positive, follow the veterinarian's treatment and activity instructions carefully. Do not rely on internet protocols or attempt to substitute routine preventive medication for a veterinary treatment plan.

## A practical Florida heartworm routine

For most dog households, the routine can stay straightforward:

- choose prevention with your veterinarian;
- give or schedule it on time, year-round;
- keep an accurate dose or appointment record;
- maintain annual heartworm testing as advised;
- call the veterinary team promptly after a missed dose or prevention gap;
- reduce standing water and mosquito breeding around the home;
- keep prevention and testing information with your dog's emergency records.

Heartworm prevention is not the most visible part of pet care. There is no daily grooming step or toy to inspect. That is exactly why the calendar matters: **a quiet, repeatable prevention habit can keep an invisible risk from becoming a much larger medical problem.**
