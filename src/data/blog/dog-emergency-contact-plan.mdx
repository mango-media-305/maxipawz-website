A pet emergency is a bad time to start searching for phone numbers, medication names, or the address of the nearest open hospital. A useful plan is deliberately simple: **save the right contacts, keep a short medical summary, make identification easy to verify, and know how you would transport your dog.**

This does not replace veterinary care or first aid training. It reduces the small delays that happen when important information is scattered across phones, cabinets, and old paperwork.

## Save your regular veterinarian

Store the clinic name, phone number, address, and normal hours in every household member's phone. If the clinic uses an after-hours partner, save that information too.

Ask what the clinic recommends when it is closed. The answer may be a specific emergency hospital, an answering service, or instructions that vary by situation.

## Identify an emergency veterinary hospital before an emergency

Do not assume the closest clinic is open around the clock or handles every type of emergency. Confirm the hospital's current location, phone number, and hours directly.

Save the route in your map app and keep the address in a written backup. If you travel with your dog, repeat this check for the destination rather than relying only on your home plan.

## Know who handles possible poisoning

FDA advises pet owners to contact their veterinarian, an emergency animal hospital, or an animal poison control service when a pet gets into something potentially dangerous.

Do not wait for symptoms when you know or strongly suspect a potentially toxic exposure. Have the product package, ingredient list, approximate amount, time of exposure, and your dog's weight available if possible. Do not induce vomiting or give a home remedy unless a veterinary professional specifically directs you.

## Keep a one-page medical summary

Your emergency file does not need to be a full medical chart. A concise summary can include:

- dog's name, age, breed or description, and current weight;
- regular veterinarian;
- current medications and doses exactly as prescribed;
- important diagnoses, allergies, or previous serious reactions;
- vaccination information when relevant;
- microchip number and registry;
- emergency contact person if you cannot be reached.

Update the page whenever medications or major health information changes.

## Make identification part of the plan

A readable tag can help a finder contact you quickly, while a registered microchip remains a backup if a collar is lost. Review our [microchip and ID checklist](/pet-guides/dog-microchip-id-emergency-preparedness) and keep a recent photo of your dog available.

For travel or evacuation, carry a copy of relevant records rather than assuming you will always have internet access.

## Plan transport, not just treatment

Think through how you would move your dog if they could not walk normally. Large dogs may require another adult, a blanket used carefully as an improvised stretcher, or equipment recommended by your veterinary team.

Keep the car reasonably ready, know where the carrier or restraint is stored, and avoid driving while trying to monitor an unrestrained dog.

## Put the plan in more than one place

A contact plan works best when it survives a dead phone or power outage. Keep a digital copy and a printed copy with your [dog first aid kit](/pet-guides/dog-first-aid-kit). Another copy can travel in an evacuation folder.

Review the information a few times a year and before major travel. Check phone numbers, hospital hours, medications, microchip registration, and the emergency contact person.

Preparedness cannot prevent every emergency. It can make the first decisions clearer when minutes and attention matter.
