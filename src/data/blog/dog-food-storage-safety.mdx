A bag of dog food can be perfectly fine at the store and still be handled poorly once it gets home. A safer routine is simple: **keep dry food cool and dry, preserve the original label and lot information, keep containers and scoops clean, refrigerate opened wet food promptly, and wash food bowls after use**.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommends keeping pet food in its original bag or container whenever practical. That advice is useful for more than freshness: the original package carries the product name, manufacturer, UPC, lot number, and best-by information that can become important during a recall or if you need to report a problem.

For South Florida households, heat and humidity make good storage habits especially worth paying attention to, but the same food-safety principles apply nationwide.

## Keep the original bag, even if you use a storage bin

A large plastic or metal bin can make feeding convenient, but pouring each new bag directly on top of old crumbs and oils is not the best routine.

FDA guidance recommends keeping dry pet food in the original bag and, if you use a secondary storage container, placing the entire bag inside the container when possible. That gives you the convenience of a bin while preserving the manufacturer's packaging and traceability.

If you choose to pour kibble directly into another container instead, the FDA advises making sure the container is clean, dry, and fitted with a secure lid. Save the product name, manufacturer, UPC, lot number, and best-by date somewhere accessible. Then clean and dry the container before adding food from the next bag.

That last step matters because residual crumbs and fats from an old bag should not simply become the bottom layer of a new one.

## Store dry food somewhere cool and dry

The garage may seem like an obvious place for a big food bag, but temperature and moisture matter.

FDA storage guidance says dry pet food and unopened canned food should be kept in a cool, dry location below 80°F. Excess heat or moisture can affect quality and nutrient stability.

In a Miami home, that usually means an air-conditioned interior pantry or another dry indoor space is a better choice than a hot garage, shed, porch, or area that regularly becomes humid.

Keep the food away from:

- direct sunlight;
- heat-producing appliances;
- damp floors or walls;
- places where water can splash into the bag or bin;
- chemicals, cleaners, pesticides, or other household products that should not be stored with food;
- areas the dog can access and open independently.

A tightly closed container can help with pests and accidental overeating, but it does not turn a hot or damp storage area into a good environment.

## Use the scoop only for pet food

The scoop is easy to overlook because it feels like part of the container. It still contacts food every day.

FDA safe-handling guidance recommends using a clean scoop, spoon, or cup dedicated to pet food rather than using the dog's bowl as the scoop. Wash and dry measuring utensils after use.

A practical system is to keep one clearly designated scoop with the food, wash it routinely, and return it only when completely dry. Avoid dropping a wet scoop into a dry-food container, where added moisture is exactly what you are trying to prevent.

If several people feed the dog, a measured scoop also makes portions easier to keep consistent. Our [feeding and hydration guide](/pet-guides/feeding-and-hydration) covers the broader routine around portions, fresh water, and observing the individual dog.

## Wash food bowls instead of continually topping them off

A bowl can look empty without being clean.

FDA guidance recommends washing and drying pet food bowls after each use and washing water bowls daily. That helps remove food residue rather than letting a new serving sit on top of old oils, saliva, and crumbs.

For households that rotate between wet and dry foods, bowl hygiene becomes even more important because moist residue can remain after the visible food is gone.

Choose bowls that are easy to clean thoroughly and inspect them for deep scratches, cracks, chips, peeling coatings, or damaged seams. A product that is difficult to clean or no longer has a sound surface may be ready for replacement rather than another round of scrubbing.

That is the same maintenance mindset we recommend in [choosing everyday dog products](/pet-guides/choosing-the-right-product): ease of cleaning and condition over time are part of product suitability, not afterthoughts.

## Handle canned and pouched food differently

Once canned or pouched food is opened, treat the unused portion as perishable.

FDA guidance says unused or leftover canned and pouched pet food should be promptly refrigerated or discarded, with the refrigerator at 40°F or below. Cover refrigerated food tightly.

Do not leave a partially used can or pouch sitting at room temperature for an undefined period simply because the dog may come back to it later. Follow the manufacturer's storage directions for that specific product, including any instructions about how long an opened portion should be kept refrigerated.

If food smells unusual, looks spoiled, has damaged or swollen packaging, or is associated with illness, stop feeding it and keep the package information available while you contact the veterinarian, manufacturer, or FDA as appropriate.

## Do not mix old and new food without cleaning the container

The "top it off" habit is convenient: when the bin gets low, open a new bag and pour it over whatever remains.

A cleaner approach is to finish the old batch, wash the container, allow it to dry fully, and then add the next bag. FDA specifically recommends washing and drying a storage container between bags when kibble has been poured directly into it.

This also creates a clean break between lot numbers. If a recall or quality issue occurs, it is much easier to know what food the dog actually ate when two or three bags have not been mixed together in one bin.

## Keep food and treats secure from the dog

Storage safety also means preventing accidental access.

A dog that opens a food bin or finds an entire bag of treats can consume far more than the planned portion. The FDA recommends storing food and treats in a secure location pets cannot access freely.

This becomes especially relevant around large bags, soft treat pouches, bulk chews, or anything with packaging a determined dog can tear open. Secure the storage area rather than relying on the dog to ignore a strong food smell.

The same rule applies to human-food events and trash. Our [dog BBQ safety guide](/pet-guides/dog-bbq-safety) explains why food-scented waste, bones, skewers, and unsafe ingredients deserve physical barriers rather than verbal reminders alone.

## Save the information you would need during a recall

Before throwing away a bag, ask: if there were a recall tomorrow, could you identify exactly what you fed?

Useful information includes:

- brand and product name;
- flavor or formula;
- package size;
- UPC;
- lot or production code;
- best-by or expiration date;
- manufacturer contact information.

Keeping the original packaging is the easiest way to preserve that information. If the bag cannot fit your storage setup, photograph the label and lot information before discarding it and keep those images until that food is finished.

## A simple dog-food storage reset

You do not need a designer pantry to improve food safety. Start with five practical checks:

**Location:** Is the food in a cool, dry indoor place rather than a hot or humid storage area?

**Packaging:** Do you still have the original bag or all the information needed to identify the lot?

**Container:** Is it clean, fully dry, intact, and closed securely?

**Tools:** Are the scoop and bowls being washed and dried routinely?

**Leftovers:** Are opened wet foods promptly refrigerated according to label directions rather than left out indefinitely?

Those habits protect freshness, make recalls easier to manage, reduce residue buildup, and make the feeding routine more consistent.

## Good storage is part of good feeding

Food safety does not end when you choose a formula. It continues through the bag, bin, scoop, bowl, refrigerator, and cleanup routine.

Keep original information. Store dry food cool and dry. Avoid endlessly topping off dirty bins. Wash feeding tools. Refrigerate opened wet food promptly. Secure treats and bulk food from unsupervised access. And when something about a product seems wrong, stop feeding it and keep the package details available.

That is a small amount of routine for a much more organized feeding system.
