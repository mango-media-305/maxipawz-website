Miami rain can turn one normal dog walk into a route full of puddles, saturated grass, drainage areas, and standing water. Most wet walks end with nothing more serious than muddy paws. But there is one bacterial disease worth understanding because **contaminated water and soil are part of how dogs can be exposed: leptospirosis**.

The practical response is not to make every puddle frightening. It is to build a few useful habits: **keep your dog from drinking unknown outdoor water, use extra caution around floodwater and stagnant water, bring fresh drinking water, keep routine veterinary care current, and know which signs after a possible exposure deserve a prompt call to your veterinarian.**

## What leptospirosis is — and why water matters

Leptospirosis is caused by bacteria in the genus *Leptospira*. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention explains that infected animals can shed the bacteria in urine, which can then contaminate water or soil and remain in the environment for weeks to months under suitable conditions.

Dogs can be exposed through direct contact with infected urine or through contaminated water and soil. Wildlife and rodents can be part of that environmental cycle, which is one reason leptospirosis is not only a "farm dog" problem.

Current CDC guidance says almost every dog can be at risk whether the dog lives in a city, suburb, or rural area. Exposure can occur around contaminated outdoor water, wildlife, rodents, and environments where dogs mix with other dogs.

That distinction matters in South Florida. A dog does not need to hike deep into the Everglades to have an outdoor lifestyle. Neighborhood walks, yards, parks, stormwater areas, boarding facilities, and post-rain puddles are ordinary parts of urban and suburban life.

## Heavy rain and flooding change the exposure picture

The Florida Department of Health notes that leptospirosis risk can increase after hurricanes, flooding, or heavy rainfall because animal urine can contaminate water and soil. CDC guidance makes the same connection between heavy rain, flooding, stagnant water, and leptospirosis risk.

That does **not** mean every puddle after a Miami thunderstorm contains dangerous bacteria. You cannot reliably identify leptospirosis contamination by color, smell, or a quick look at the water. The useful decision is therefore about exposure rather than visual diagnosis.

After heavy rain:

- redirect your dog from drinking puddles, ditches, or other standing water;
- keep your dog from playing in floodwater or water with an unknown contamination history;
- bring fresh water so thirst does not make the nearest puddle more tempting;
- use a leash where you need more control around drainage areas or roadside water;
- keep trash, outdoor pet food, and other rodent attractants managed around the home.

For post-storm planning, our [Hurricane Preparedness for Dogs guide](/pet-guides/hurricane-preparedness-for-dogs) covers evacuation supplies, records, identification, medication planning, and the broader hazards that can appear after a storm.

## Standing water is not the only possible exposure

It is easy to reduce leptospirosis to "do not drink puddles," but that is only one part of the picture.

CDC guidance lists direct contact with infected urine or contaminated water among important exposure routes and notes that bite wounds or skin damage from infected animals can also matter. Rodents, wildlife, and other animals can carry the bacteria without making the source obvious to a dog owner.

That is why a dog who lives in a condo or walks on city sidewalks is not automatically outside the risk conversation. Urban rodents, shared outdoor spaces, dog parks, boarding, and ordinary neighborhood wildlife can still create opportunities for exposure.

The best prevention plan combines environmental habits with veterinary preventive care rather than relying on one perfect rule about where a dog is allowed to walk.

## Leptospirosis vaccination is now core guidance

One of the biggest changes in the veterinary conversation around leptospirosis is vaccination guidance.

The American Animal Hospital Association updated its canine vaccination guidance in 2024 to designate leptospirosis vaccination as a **core vaccine**. AAHA's explanation emphasizes that dogs in urban, suburban, and rural settings can be exposed and that the old assumption that mainly large rural dogs are at risk no longer fits the evidence.

That does not mean an online article should decide your individual dog's vaccine schedule. Age, current health, previous vaccine history, prior reactions, travel, local disease patterns, and other medical considerations belong in a conversation with your veterinarian.

If you are unsure whether your dog is currently protected, ask your veterinary team a specific question at the next appointment: **"Is my dog's leptospirosis vaccination current under today's guidance, and when is the next dose due?"**

Vaccination also does not make environmental habits irrelevant. CDC notes that vaccines do not cover every strain, so avoiding likely exposure and following veterinary guidance still matter.

## Build a rainy-day routine that is easy to repeat

A useful prevention routine should fit a normal Tuesday, not just a hurricane warning.

Before a wet-weather walk, fill a portable bottle or bowl with fresh water if the outing will be long enough that your dog may become thirsty. During the walk, redirect from puddle drinking and standing-water play without turning the entire outing into a constant correction session.

Afterward, clean and dry paws as your dog's normal grooming needs require and take a quick look for cuts, abrasions, or irritation. Our [rainy-season paw-care guide](/pet-guides/rainy-season-dog-paw-care-miami) shows how to make that cleanup and inspection one repeatable routine.

## What can leptospirosis look like in a dog?

Leptospirosis can be difficult to recognize from symptoms alone because the signs overlap with many other illnesses. CDC guidance lists possible early signs in dogs such as:

- fever;
- unusual tiredness or reduced activity;
- loss of appetite;
- muscle or joint pain;
- changes in urination;
- eye or nasal discharge;
- jaundice, or yellowing of the eyes or skin.

As illness progresses, some dogs can develop vomiting, dehydration, back pain, kidney injury, liver involvement, or other serious complications.

A dog does not need to show every sign for the situation to deserve veterinary attention. Likewise, vomiting after a rainy walk does not automatically mean leptospirosis.

What makes the history useful is the combination: **new illness plus a plausible exposure**, such as drinking stagnant water, contact with floodwater, significant wildlife or rodent exposure, or another situation your veterinarian considers relevant.

If your dog becomes noticeably ill after a possible exposure, contact your veterinarian promptly and tell them about the water, weather, wildlife, or travel history instead of trying to diagnose the disease from symptoms at home.

## When the situation needs urgent veterinary care

Leptospirosis can affect the kidneys, liver, and other organs, so significant illness should not be managed as a wait-and-see home project.

Seek prompt veterinary guidance if a dog develops concerning symptoms such as repeated vomiting, marked weakness, collapse, significant dehydration, yellowing of the eyes or skin, major changes in urination, or other rapidly worsening signs—especially after a plausible exposure.

The veterinarian may recommend diagnostic testing based on the dog's symptoms, vaccination history, examination, and exposure history. Treatment decisions, including antibiotics and supportive care, belong with the veterinary team.

Avoid giving human medication or leftover antibiotics unless a veterinarian specifically directs you to do so. Starting the wrong medication or dose can create a second problem and may complicate evaluation.

## Leptospirosis is also a household-health issue

Leptospirosis is zoonotic, meaning it can affect people as well as animals. If a veterinarian suspects or diagnoses leptospirosis in your dog, follow the clinic's instructions carefully for medication, follow-up testing, cleaning, and handling urine or other potentially contaminated material.

CDC advises avoiding contact with urine or blood from an infected animal before treatment is complete, covering cuts, using protective gloves or other appropriate barriers when contact cannot be avoided, and washing hands after handling animals or items that may have urine or feces on them.

Do not improvise a home decontamination protocol from social media. Ask the veterinary team exactly how they want bedding, floors, yard areas, and accidents handled for your dog's case.

If a person in the household is concerned about exposure or develops illness after contact with a diagnosed animal, contact a healthcare professional and mention the leptospirosis concern. Veterinary care for the dog and medical care for people are separate decisions, but sharing the exposure history helps both teams.

## After a storm, keep the plan broader than one disease

Floodwater and post-storm standing water can carry multiple hazards, not just leptospirosis. Debris, sewage, chemicals, electrical hazards, displaced wildlife, parasites, and contaminated natural water can all change the risk of a familiar neighborhood.

CDC pet-emergency guidance specifically recommends avoiding stagnant water after flooding and not allowing pets to play in or drink contaminated water.

That is a good reason to keep dogs leashed when normal routes have changed, bring known-safe drinking water, and wait for official guidance before treating a flooded park, canal edge, or trail as routine again.

If a storm forces a change in where you walk, choose the safest available route rather than trying to preserve the dog's usual route at all costs. Routine matters, but conditions come first.

## The goal is prevention without panic

Leptospirosis deserves attention because the disease can be serious, the bacteria can be carried by animals around both cities and rural areas, and water or soil contaminated with urine can become an exposure route.

But useful prevention is straightforward: **keep vaccination conversations current, discourage drinking from unknown standing water, use extra caution after heavy rain and flooding, manage obvious rodent attractants, bring fresh water on outings, and call your veterinarian when illness and a plausible exposure occur together.**

For Miami dog families, those habits fit naturally into the same rainy-season routine already used for paw care, hurricane preparedness, hydration, and water safety. For dog owners elsewhere, the local weather may look different, but the underlying approach remains useful anywhere dogs share outdoor spaces with wildlife, rodents, and natural water.
