Being able to spend some time alone is a useful life skill for many dogs, but it should be built around the individual dog rather than a fixed number of hours. Start with absences your dog can handle calmly, make departures predictable, and pay attention to what actually happens after you leave.

## Practice before you need a long absence

Cornell recommends gradual desensitization for separation-related anxiety. Begin with very short separations, such as stepping out of sight and returning before your dog becomes distressed. Increase difficulty slowly only when the current step is comfortable.

Avoid turning every departure into a dramatic event. A familiar walk, bathroom break and quiet transition can make the routine easier to understand.

## Prepare the space, not just the dog

Choose an area that is comfortable, temperature-appropriate and free of hazards. Remove trash, cords, medications, foods and objects your dog might swallow. Fresh water should remain accessible.

A crate is appropriate only for a dog that is already comfortable using it. Cornell specifically warns against forcing a dog to remain crated alone when confinement triggers excessive barking, pacing, panting, drooling, elimination or other distress. Some dogs do better in a larger dog-proofed area.

## Use enrichment thoughtfully

A safe food puzzle, chew or scent activity can give a relaxed dog something appropriate to do. ASPCA recommends active supervision for DIY enrichment and removing items a dog attempts to ingest. That means a homemade activity that works while you are watching is not automatically appropriate for unsupervised use.

Before leaving any product with your dog, inspect it for damage and consider your dog's chewing and swallowing habits. Our [product-safety guide](/product-safety) can help with that check.

## Know what distress can look like

Separation-related distress can include barking or howling, pacing, house soiling, destructive behavior and intense stress around departure cues. A camera can help you observe behavior without assuming that a damaged cushion automatically means anxiety.

If your dog panics, attempts to escape, injures themselves, cannot settle, or shows persistent distress, contact your veterinarian. A qualified behavior professional may also be part of the plan. Punishment after you return does not teach a frightened dog how to feel safe alone.

## Make your return useful information

Notice whether your dog is relaxed, whether enrichment was used normally and whether the environment is intact. Those observations help you decide whether the next absence should stay the same, become shorter or progress gradually.

A successful home-alone routine is not about proving that a dog can tolerate the longest possible absence. It is about creating a predictable setup in which your dog can rest safely and you can recognize early when the plan is not working.
