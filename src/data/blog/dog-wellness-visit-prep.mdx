A wellness visit is most useful when your veterinarian sees more than the dog who happens to be standing in the exam room. A short record of diet, medications, behavior, mobility and recent changes can help the veterinary team understand what daily life actually looks like.

## Bring the current record, not the record you remember

Collect vaccination records from other clinics, recent test results if relevant, microchip information and a list of medications and supplements. Include product names, strengths and how you actually give them. Photos of labels can be useful when carrying every container is impractical.

Do not stop or change a prescription before the appointment unless the prescribing veterinarian instructs you to do so.

## Write down food before you arrive

Record the exact food, approximate daily amount, treats, chews and supplements your dog receives. If several people feed the dog, compare notes first. This gives the veterinarian a better picture than simply saying “about two cups.”

AAHA includes nutrition and weight management among the subjects addressed during routine care. If you have been tracking weight or body condition, bring those observations too.

## Note changes that are easy to forget in the exam room

Think through the previous weeks: appetite, drinking, bathroom habits, sleep, activity, coughing, scratching, mobility and behavior. Write down what changed, when you first noticed it and whether it is becoming more or less frequent.

A short video of an intermittent limp, cough or unusual behavior may provide useful context if the behavior does not happen at the clinic. It does not replace an examination.

## Describe your dog's real lifestyle

Preventive recommendations depend partly on exposure. Tell the veterinary team about boarding, daycare, travel, swimming, wildlife contact, dog parks and time spent outdoors. CDC recommends routine veterinary care and discussion of parasite prevention; lifestyle details help make those conversations specific.

Ask what preventive plan fits your dog's age, health and exposure rather than copying another dog's schedule.

## Put your top questions first

Appointments move quickly. Write your two or three most important questions at the top of the list. Topics might include dental care, body condition, behavior, mobility, vaccines, parasite prevention or a change you noticed at home.

AAHA recommends routine veterinary visits at least annually for pets generally, with frequency individualized by life stage and health. Follow your own veterinarian's recommended schedule.

## Leave with a plan you understand

Before leaving, confirm what should happen next: home care, tests, medication instructions, monitoring, follow-up timing and which changes should prompt an earlier call. Ask for clarification if a direction is unclear.

The goal of preparation is not to diagnose your dog at home. It is to give the veterinary team accurate information so the visit can focus on decisions that fit your individual dog.
