A sunny sidewalk can look perfectly normal and still be uncomfortable—or unsafe—for a dog’s paws. In **Miami and South Florida**, long periods of heat, strong sun, warm evenings, concrete, asphalt, and exposed sand make the walking surface itself part of the decision about when and where to walk.

The most useful approach is not to rely on one air-temperature number. **Check the surface, choose cooler hours, build shade into the route, carry water, and watch the individual dog in front of you.**

## Why pavement can be hotter than the air

Air temperature is only one part of what your dog experiences. Dark asphalt and other sun-exposed surfaces absorb solar energy and can become much hotter than the surrounding air.

Miami-Dade Animal Services specifically warns that **cement, asphalt, and even sand** can become hot enough to burn paws and can also contribute to overheating. The American Kennel Club similarly notes that pavement can reach temperatures far above the air temperature during sustained hot weather.

That means a weather app saying the air is warm—but not extreme—does not automatically make the sidewalk comfortable.

## The best walking times in Miami are usually early and late

Miami-Dade recommends walking and exercising dogs in the **early morning and late evening** instead of during the middle of the day. Cornell also recommends limiting activity to cooler parts of the day and notes that late afternoon can remain especially hot.

For a practical Miami routine:

- make the longer walk the early-morning walk when possible;
- keep mid-day outings brief and functional on hot days;
- use evening walks after surfaces have had time to cool;
- choose routes with trees, grass, shade, or covered areas;
- shorten the route when humidity and direct sun make conditions feel more demanding.

A late-evening walk is not automatically safe simply because the sun is lower. Asphalt and concrete can retain heat, so the surface still deserves a quick check.

## Use a simple surface check before committing to the route

Cornell recommends using your own hand or bare foot as a practical surface check. If you cannot comfortably keep your hand or bare foot on the pavement for roughly **10 seconds**, the surface is likely too hot for your dog to walk on comfortably.

This is useful because it checks the exact surface your dog is about to use rather than relying only on the reported air temperature.

Check again when conditions change:

- moving from shade into direct sun;
- switching from grass to asphalt;
- entering a parking lot;
- walking onto concrete near buildings;
- reaching a beach or sandy path;
- walking later in the day after surfaces have absorbed hours of sun.

The test is a practical screening tool, not a guarantee. Your dog’s paw condition, age, gait, activity level, and individual sensitivity still matter.

## Do not treat 85°F as a universal cutoff

You may see **85°F** mentioned in pavement-safety discussions. AKC notes that when air temperatures are around 85°F or higher and the pavement has not had a chance to cool, the ground may be unsafe for walking.

That is useful context, but it should not become a rigid rule. A shaded sidewalk, a black parking lot, beach sand, artificial turf, and concrete can behave very differently under the same reported air temperature.

A better decision combines:

1. current heat and humidity;
2. direct sunlight;
3. the actual walking surface;
4. how long that surface has been heating;
5. shade availability;
6. your dog’s normal response to heat.

## Choose cooler surfaces whenever you can

If the sidewalk is too hot, changing the route can be more useful than trying to push through the same walk.

Look for:

- grass instead of asphalt;
- shaded sidewalks instead of exposed parking lots;
- tree-covered neighborhood routes;
- shorter loops closer to home;
- indoor enrichment when outdoor conditions are poor.

Miami-Dade’s heat guidance encourages keeping pets out of the strongest mid-day heat, and the County’s Animal Services guidance specifically recommends shade and avoiding hot surfaces.

## Paw protection can help, but it does not make heat irrelevant

Dog boots can create a barrier between paw pads and a hot surface, and Miami-Dade lists dog boots as an option when walking during cooler-hour alternatives is not possible.

If you use boots:

- choose the correct size;
- introduce them before the day you actually need them;
- watch for rubbing or slipping;
- inspect paws after the walk;
- remember that boots protect paws from the surface but **do not prevent whole-body overheating**.

A dog can still become overheated while wearing excellent paw protection if the air is hot and humid or the activity is too demanding.

## Carry water and build in shade breaks

Hot-pavement safety and hydration belong in the same routine. Miami-Dade recommends bringing water on walks, providing shade, and taking breaks during warm-weather outings.

Our dedicated [Dog Hydration in Miami Heat](/pet-guides/dog-hydration-miami-heat) guide goes deeper into water, shade, humidity, and heat warning signs, while the broader [Dog Walking and Travel Essentials](/pet-guides/walk-and-travel) guide covers the equipment side of the routine.

A simple hot-weather walking kit can include:

- clean drinking water;
- a portable bowl or drinking container;
- waste bags;
- current identification;
- a properly fitted leash, collar, or harness;
- paw protection when appropriate.

## Watch the dog, not just the pavement

Paw burns are not the only concern. Cornell notes that heatstroke can occur when dogs exercise in hot and humid conditions, and all dogs can be affected.

Early signs that a dog may be struggling with the heat can include:

- heavy or increasing panting;
- repeatedly seeking shade;
- slowing down or refusing to continue;
- drooling;
- restlessness;
- wanting to sit or lie down.

Dogs with short muzzles, older dogs, puppies, overweight dogs, dogs with heart or breathing conditions, and some thick- or dark-coated dogs may require extra caution.

If a dog develops severe signs such as difficulty breathing, weakness, confusion, vomiting, seizures, or collapse, heatstroke is a medical emergency and veterinary care is needed promptly.

## Check paws after warm-weather walks

After a walk, take a quick look at each paw. Changes worth paying attention to include:

- unusual redness;
- tenderness;
- limping or reluctance to bear weight;
- licking or chewing at the paws;
- blisters, peeling, or damaged pads.

Do not continue a normal walking routine over an injured paw. Significant burns, open wounds, persistent limping, or obvious pain should be evaluated by a veterinarian.

## A practical Miami walking decision

Before heading out, use a simple sequence:

1. **Check the weather.** Heat and humidity both matter.
2. **Choose the cooler part of the day.** Early morning is often the best opportunity for a longer walk.
3. **Touch the surface.** If you cannot comfortably tolerate it for about 10 seconds, choose another surface or wait.
4. **Look for shade and grass.** Build them into the route rather than hoping to find them later.
5. **Bring water.** Even a familiar neighborhood walk can feel different in Miami heat.
6. **Watch your dog.** Shorten or stop the outing when your dog shows that conditions are becoming too demanding.

## The best Miami dog walk is the one that fits the conditions

There is no prize for completing the usual route when the sidewalk is too hot. In South Florida, changing the time, shortening the walk, crossing to the shaded side of the street, choosing grass, or moving exercise indoors can all be sensible choices.

The goal is simple: **protect the paws, reduce unnecessary heat exposure, and make the walking routine sustainable throughout Miami’s long warm season.**
