For dogs in Miami, heartworm prevention is not a once-a-year warm-weather task. The practical routine is **year-round veterinarian-directed prevention, regular heartworm testing, consistent dosing or injection appointments, and mosquito-source reduction around the home**.

That approach matters locally because Miami-Dade has a subtropical environment with many mosquito species, and the county specifically lists **dog heartworm** among mosquito-transmitted diseases relevant to the area. But this is not only a South Florida issue: the American Heartworm Society says heartworm disease has been diagnosed in dogs in every U.S. state and recommends year-round prevention.

This guide is educational, not a medication selector. The right preventive depends on your dog's age, weight, health history, current parasite-control products, travel, and testing status. Your veterinarian should make that decision with you.

## Why mosquitoes are the heartworm link

Dogs do not catch heartworms directly from another dog. Transmission requires an infected mosquito. The mosquito picks up immature heartworms while feeding on an infected animal and can later transmit infective larvae when it bites another susceptible dog.

That is why a dog can be exposed during ordinary life: a backyard break, an evening walk, time at a park, a patio visit, or travel to another mosquito-prone area. A dog does not need to spend hours near a swamp to have meaningful exposure.

The American Heartworm Society also emphasizes an important timing point: preventive medications are designed to eliminate susceptible immature stages acquired during an earlier exposure window. That makes **giving prevention on schedule** more important than trying to predict which particular day mosquitoes were present.

## In Miami, think year-round rather than "mosquito season"

Florida does have periods of heavier mosquito activity, and University of Florida experts note particularly active conditions from late spring into early fall. But South Florida's warm, humid climate and frequent rain make a narrow seasonal approach unreliable.

Miami-Dade County says its subtropical environment supports dozens of mosquito species and operates an ongoing mosquito-control program. Heavy rain and storms can also create new breeding sites in containers that were dry days earlier.

For dog owners, that means the safer question is not, "Are mosquitoes bad this week?" It is, **"Is my dog's preventive plan current all year?"**

The American Heartworm Society and Companion Animal Parasite Council both recommend year-round heartworm prevention for dogs. CAPC's January 2026 heartworm guidance also recommends annual testing for both heartworm antigen and circulating microfilariae.

## Prevention and testing are two different jobs

It is easy to assume that a dog receiving prevention no longer needs heartworm testing. Current veterinary-parasite guidance says otherwise.

Preventive medication lowers risk, while testing checks whether infection has occurred. Dogs can miss a dose, spit out or vomit medication, receive a product incorrectly, arrive from another household with an uncertain history, or have gaps that are easy to forget months later.

That is why annual testing remains part of routine care even for dogs whose owners are consistent.

If you have adopted a dog, moved to Florida, changed preventives, or do not know when the last dose was given, avoid guessing. Ask the veterinarian what testing and restart schedule is appropriate for that specific dog.

## What to do if a dose or appointment was missed

A missed heartworm preventive is a reason to act promptly, not to improvise.

Do not automatically double a dose, combine products, or restart an old medication without checking the label and speaking with the prescribing veterinarian. The appropriate next step depends on the product, how late the dose is, the dog's previous testing status, and how long protection may have lapsed.

The American Heartworm Society advises owners with a lapse to consult their veterinarian, restart prevention as directed, and plan follow-up testing on the veterinarian's timeline because very recent infections may not be detectable immediately.

A useful household habit is to make prevention hard to forget: put the next administration or clinic date on a shared calendar, keep refill reminders active, and record the date immediately after the medication is given.

## Mosquito control helps, but it cannot replace heartworm prevention

Reducing mosquitoes around the home is worthwhile. It lowers nuisance biting and can reduce exposure to mosquito-borne disease. It is **not** a substitute for preventive medication.

Miami-Dade's "Drain and Cover" guidance recommends regularly checking places where rain or irrigation water collects, including flowerpots, toys, gutters, pool covers, coolers, trash containers, birdbaths, and pet water bowls. The county also recommends cleaning and changing water in pet bowls regularly and preventing containers from holding water long enough for mosquitoes to breed.

After major rain or tropical weather, walk the property again. A bucket, folded tarp, planter saucer, dog toy, or clogged gutter can become a water-holding site even if it was dry earlier in the week.

If mosquitoes are unusually heavy around a Miami-Dade property, the county allows residents to request mosquito-control inspection through 311.

## Be cautious with mosquito repellents on dogs

Human insect repellent is not automatically pet-safe. Do not apply DEET, essential-oil mixtures, yard pesticides, or another person's flea/tick product to a dog just because it repels insects.

If mosquito repellency is needed in addition to heartworm prevention, ask your veterinarian which **dog-labeled product** is appropriate and how it fits with the dog's existing flea, tick, and parasite medications. Using multiple products without checking active ingredients can create unnecessary risk.

This is a broader product-safety principle: the label, species, dose, route, weight range, and intended use matter more than a marketing phrase such as "natural" or "pet friendly." Our [dog product selection guide](/pet-guides/choosing-the-right-product) explains that decision process in more detail.

## Do not wait for symptoms to decide whether prevention matters

Heartworm disease can be present before obvious signs appear. As disease progresses, dogs may develop coughing, reduced exercise tolerance, tiredness, appetite or weight changes, breathing difficulty, or signs related to heart and lung damage.

Those signs are not specific to heartworm disease and should not be used for home diagnosis. A dog with coughing, breathing difficulty, collapse, unusual weakness, or other concerning changes needs veterinary evaluation based on severity and the dog's medical history.

Prevention is valuable precisely because waiting for visible illness is the wrong strategy. Heartworm treatment is more complex than routine prevention and can require substantial veterinary management and activity restriction.

## A practical Miami heartworm routine

The best routine is intentionally boring. Confirm that your dog is on a veterinarian-recommended preventive and give it exactly as directed. Keep the yearly heartworm-testing visit on the calendar. Check the yard after rain for standing water. Keep mosquito screens and drainage in good repair. Review the plan whenever your dog changes weight, starts another parasite product, travels, is adopted with an uncertain history, or misses a preventive dose.

For hot-weather outdoor planning, pair parasite prevention with our [dog hydration in Miami heat guide](/pet-guides/dog-hydration-miami-heat). For emergency records and veterinary contacts, keep a current [dog first aid kit](/pet-guides/dog-first-aid-kit) ready at home and in the car.

## The bottom line

Heartworm risk is tied to mosquitoes, but mosquito avoidance alone is not enough. For dogs in Miami, the strongest routine combines **year-round veterinary prevention, regular testing, on-time administration, and sensible mosquito-source reduction**.

If your dog's prevention history is uncertain, a dose was missed, or you are considering changing products, contact your veterinarian rather than filling the gap with an over-the-counter guess. A simple, documented prevention plan is much easier to manage than heartworm disease after infection has taken hold.
