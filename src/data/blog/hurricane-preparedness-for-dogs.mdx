For dog owners in **Miami, Miami-Dade County, and the rest of South Florida**, hurricane preparation is part of normal life with a pet. The useful time to decide where your dog will go, what supplies are needed, and who can help is **before** a storm is close enough to create last-minute pressure.

The same framework is useful for dog owners anywhere hurricanes or tropical storms can affect travel, utilities, flooding, or evacuation. Your dog should be included in the household emergency plan from the beginning rather than added after everything else is packed.

## Make the plan before the forecast becomes urgent

Start by deciding what your household will do if local officials recommend evacuation or sheltering in place.

CDC hurricane guidance recommends planning ahead, preparing supplies, identifying shelters and evacuation routes, and pre-identifying a pet-friendly hotel, shelter, friend, or relative where pets can go if evacuation becomes necessary.

For your dog, write down:

- your primary evacuation destination;
- at least one backup destination;
- how your dog will travel;
- who can help if you are away from home;
- your regular veterinarian’s contact information;
- an emergency veterinary contact near your likely destination;
- where the pet emergency kit is stored.

Do not assume the plan from last hurricane season is still current. Hotels change policies, household members move, medications change, and local shelter operations can change from one event to another.

## Build a dog emergency kit that can leave with you

A useful emergency kit should be packed so it can travel with the household, not spread across several rooms where essential items need to be found during an evacuation.

CDC’s current pet disaster-kit guidance includes veterinary and ownership records, recent photographs, microchip information, food, water, medications, bowls, a leash, collar with ID, harness, an appropriately sized carrier, bedding or a blanket, first-aid materials, and cleaning supplies.

CDC currently recommends preparing a **two-week supply of food and water for each animal**, along with a two-week medication supply when applicable. Follow medication storage instructions and ask your veterinarian how to plan for prescriptions that have special storage or refill requirements.

A practical dog kit may include:

- food in protected containers;
- drinking water reserved for the dog;
- food and water bowls;
- a manual can opener when needed;
- prescription medications and written instructions;
- current veterinary and vaccination records;
- proof of ownership or registration information;
- microchip number and registration-company details;
- collar with ID, leash, harness, and carrier or other appropriate transport equipment;
- waste bags, paper towels, and cleanup supplies;
- a first-aid kit;
- a familiar toy, towel, or blanket;
- recent photos of your dog;
- written household contact information.

Keep documents in a water-resistant or waterproof container and rotate food, water, medications, batteries, and other dated supplies instead of treating the kit as something you pack once and forget.

## Check identification before hurricane season

Severe weather can damage fences and doors and alter the scents and landmarks a dog normally uses to understand familiar surroundings. CDC notes that pets may become confused and lost after emergencies.

Before hurricane season—and again when a storm may affect your area—check:

- the phone number on the ID tag;
- tag readability;
- the tag attachment ring;
- collar condition and fit;
- microchip registration status;
- the phone numbers and address associated with the microchip;
- recent photos that clearly show the dog.

## Know Miami-Dade’s pet-friendly evacuation process

Miami-Dade County operates pet-friendly evacuation centers during emergencies, but **centers are announced as needed**. That means a static blog post should never tell you that a particular location will definitely be open for the next storm.

When an evacuation may be necessary, check current Miami-Dade emergency information for the centers actually activated for that event.

Miami-Dade currently states that pre-registration is not required for its pet-friendly evacuation centers. Its published requirements include proof of residence within an evacuation zone, current medical and vaccination records, a visible Miami-Dade County dog license, supplies for the owner and pet, and maintaining control of pets with a crate, leash, or other appropriate method. The County also notes a household pet limit for these centers.

Those details can change, so verify the official requirements each time rather than relying exclusively on this article.

## Keep a primary destination and a backup

Even outside Miami-Dade, do not assume a general emergency shelter accepts pets. CDC notes that many evacuation centers do not allow pets and recommends planning alternatives in advance.

Possible options include:

- an officially designated pet-friendly evacuation center;
- a pet-friendly hotel along the evacuation route;
- friends or relatives outside the affected area;
- boarding facilities;
- veterinary hospitals when appropriate;
- local animal-welfare resources identified by emergency officials.

Confirm pet policies directly. A property being “pet friendly” during normal operations does not guarantee that the same rules apply during an emergency.

## Practice transportation before you need to evacuate

A hurricane evacuation should not be your dog’s first experience with a carrier, vehicle restraint, unfamiliar car setup, or several hours of travel.

CDC recommends helping pets become comfortable with carriers and practicing transportation and household evacuation routines ahead of time.

Practice can be simple:

1. allow your dog to investigate the carrier or restraint equipment calmly;
2. practice getting into the vehicle;
3. take short drives using the same setup you would use during evacuation;
4. practice gathering the emergency kit and records;
5. make sure every household member knows where the dog’s equipment is stored.

## Prepare a safe indoor area if you stay home

If local authorities advise that staying home is appropriate, choose the safest practical interior area for the household and include your dog in that setup.

CDC hurricane guidance advises staying indoors, away from windows, keeping emergency supplies accessible, monitoring official updates, and remaining ready to leave if authorities later order evacuation or if the home becomes unsafe.

Miami-Dade’s pet guidance advises keeping pets together with the household and using a crate or carrier in a safe area.

Before conditions deteriorate, bring into the safe area:

- the dog’s emergency kit;
- drinking water;
- medications;
- leash and harness;
- carrier when applicable;
- bedding or a familiar comfort item;
- cleanup supplies;
- a phone charger or battery pack for access to emergency information.

Keep doors and windows secure and make sure the area does not give the dog access to household chemicals, unsafe plants, damaged materials, or tight hiding places that would make evacuation difficult.

## Plan hydration as an emergency supply, not an afterthought

Water should be part of the kit before a hurricane affects utilities or travel.

Do not plan on simply filling the dog’s bowl from whatever source is available after a storm. If local officials issue drinking-water guidance, apply that information to water being used for pets as well and follow the instructions provided by public-health or emergency authorities.

Use clean containers and keep food and water protected from floodwater, chemicals, debris, and damaged packaging.

For everyday and travel hydration routines, see our [feeding and hydration guide](/pet-guides/feeding-and-hydration).

## After the storm, treat familiar places as changed environments

A backyard, sidewalk, park, or neighborhood route can look familiar while containing hazards that were not there before the storm.

Possible post-storm hazards include:

- broken fencing;
- sharp debris;
- exposed wiring;
- standing or flood water;
- spilled chemicals or fuel;
- unstable branches or structures;
- displaced wildlife;
- spoiled food;
- contaminated bowls or outdoor containers.

CDC recommends keeping pets leashed or in carriers after an emergency because landmarks and scents may have changed, and avoiding unsafe or contaminated water.

Do not immediately return to normal off-leash routines simply because the rain or wind has stopped. Inspect the environment first.

## Watch your dog after the emergency

A meaningful change in appetite, drinking, mobility, breathing, comfort, skin, digestion, or behavior after a storm can justify veterinary attention, especially when there may have been exposure to heat, contaminated water, debris, injury, or missed medication.

This guide cannot determine whether a post-storm change is medically significant. Contact a veterinarian when your dog appears injured, ill, painful, unusually weak, or otherwise concerning.

## Use a final Miami hurricane checklist

Before hurricane season, and again when a tropical system may affect South Florida, verify:

- **Emergency kit:** packed and recently checked.
- **Food and water:** sufficient reserves are ready.
- **Medications:** current supply and instructions are packed.
- **Records:** vaccination, medical, ownership, and microchip information are protected.
- **Identification:** ID tag and microchip contact information are current.
- **Walking gear:** collar, leash, harness, and carrier or transport equipment are ready.
- **Evacuation destination:** primary and backup options are identified.
- **Miami-Dade information:** current evacuation orders and pet-friendly center announcements can be accessed.
- **Transportation:** household members know how the dog will travel.
- **Veterinary contacts:** regular and emergency contacts are saved.
- **Practice:** the dog has experience with the carrier, vehicle, or restraint setup.

## Preparation works best when it happens early

A hurricane plan for your dog does not need to be complicated. It needs to be **current, portable, and realistic**.

Keep identification updated, maintain a practical emergency kit, know where your dog can go, practice the travel routine, and follow current instructions from Miami-Dade County and other emergency authorities when a storm threatens.
