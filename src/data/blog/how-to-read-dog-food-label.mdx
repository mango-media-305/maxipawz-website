A dog food label is easier to use when you stop treating the front of the bag as the main source of nutrition information. **Start with nutritional adequacy, intended species and life stage, calories, feeding directions, guaranteed analysis, and ingredients.**

This guide explains labels rather than prescribing a diet. Needs vary by age, body condition, activity, health, and other factors. Individual nutrition questions belong with your veterinarian.

## Find nutritional adequacy first

AAFCO identifies the nutritional adequacy statement as a key part of selecting pet food. It tells you whether a product is intended to be complete and balanced and for which life stage or use.

A product intended for intermittent or supplemental feeding is not the same as a complete daily diet. Ask whether the food is for dogs, whether it is complete and balanced, which life stage is named, and whether that matches your dog.

## Read calories and feeding directions together

Feeding directions are a starting point, not a promise that every dog needs exactly that amount. Look for the calorie statement and note its unit.

If weight or body condition changes unexpectedly, review food amount, treats, activity, and health with your veterinarian. Our [body condition guide](/pet-guides/dog-body-condition-score-healthy-weight) explains why scale weight is only part of the picture.

## Understand the guaranteed analysis

The guaranteed analysis commonly includes minimum crude protein and fat and maximum fiber and moisture. It is useful, but foods with very different moisture levels are not always directly comparable from the as-fed percentages alone.

For medical diet comparisons, ask your veterinarian for the appropriate basis instead of choosing solely from the largest number.

## Ingredients are listed by weight

FDA requires proper ingredient listing, and AAFCO explains that ingredients appear in descending order by weight. The list is useful, but the first ingredient is not a complete quality score.

Use the ingredient statement especially when a veterinary professional has advised you to include or avoid a known ingredient. Do not diagnose a food allergy from a label alone.

## Look past front-of-bag claims

Product names and claims can follow specific labeling rules. Read beyond the package title to the adequacy statement, calories, ingredients, and directions.

Keep the original package or a clear photo of the lot code, best-by date, and responsible company. Those details matter if you need to check a recall or report a concern. For storage, see our [dog food storage guide](/pet-guides/dog-food-storage-safety).

## A five-minute label check

Before buying an everyday food, confirm the species and life stage, adequacy statement, calories and directions, ingredient restrictions relevant to your dog, and responsible company.

Then evaluate the food in the context of **your dog**. Appetite, stool quality, body condition, weight trend, skin and coat, activity, and medical history all give your veterinary team useful context.

A good label does not choose the food for you. It gives you better information for making that choice.
