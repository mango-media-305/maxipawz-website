A sitter may know when your dog eats and still be unsure what to do when a thunderstorm arrives, the air conditioning fails or your return flight is delayed. In South Florida, the handoff should explain who makes those decisions and where the necessary supplies are kept.

This is a planning worksheet for a carer you have chosen. It does not assume a particular service, guarantee outdoor conditions or replace your veterinarian's instructions.

## Write the ordinary day in concrete terms

Record meal times and measured portions, medication instructions, bathroom needs, rest locations and familiar cues. Use the dog's actual routine instead of broad directions such as “a little food” or “a long walk.”

Show the sitter the equipment and practice securing it together. Identify the usual exit, a backup key arrangement and any doors or gates that require an extra check. Describe handling needs and places your dog should avoid. Keep access codes in a private handoff, not a publicly shared checklist.

## Give the sitter permission to change the outing

Use the [National Weather Service Miami office](https://www.weather.gov/mfl/) for current local alerts and forecasts. A calendar date in October does not establish that an afternoon walk will be comfortable. Write down a shorter bathroom route and an indoor alternative before you leave.

Explain the routine in our [heat and hydration guide](/pet-guides/dog-hydration-miami-heat): carry drinking water, avoid hot surfaces, choose suitable conditions and shorten or stop an outing when needed. Extra water does not compensate for excessive heat. Never leave the dog waiting in a parked vehicle.

## Demonstrate the storm routine

Show the familiar quiet area and explain how your dog behaves during thunder. Leave the veterinary plan for any prescribed medication, including the exact label instructions. The sitter should not invent a dose or try a new calming product.

Our [thunderstorm guide](/pet-guides/dog-thunderstorm-anxiety-miami) can support that discussion. Tell the sitter which comfort measures the dog already accepts and which handling makes the situation harder. Do a walkthrough before travel rather than introducing the plan during a storm.

## Name a backup person and a safe destination

Miami-Dade's preparedness guidance emphasizes supplies, identification and including pets in evacuation plans. Apply that to the handoff: who can collect the dog if the primary sitter cannot reach the home? Who can transport the animal if the owner is away?

Record a destination that has actually agreed to accept the dog, with a second option. Recheck current access and admission conditions when a storm threatens; do not assume that every hotel or evacuation site accepts pets. The sitter must follow current official instructions and must not enter unsafe areas to carry out an outdated plan.

## Show the supplies instead of listing them only

Walk the sitter to the carrier or transport equipment, records, food, drinking water and medications. Use the [hurricane preparation guide](/pet-guides/hurricane-preparedness-for-dogs) to review the kit separately. Label anything that could be confused with another pet's supplies.

Add a plan for loss of cooling or an extended absence. Specify the backup person to contact and the safe place to move the dog when conditions require it. Never make “wait until I answer” the only response to an urgent problem.

## Finish with a clear communication plan

Provide your veterinarian, a confirmed emergency veterinary contact and an authorized backup decision-maker. Agree on how urgent veterinary care will be arranged if you are unreachable. Breathing difficulty, collapse or other severe signs require urgent veterinary attention.

For routine updates, request simple observations: meals, medication given, bathroom trips, activity changes and any incident. Before departure, ask the sitter to explain the weather and backup plan in their own words. Any uncertainty that appears during that conversation is something you can fix while you are still home.
