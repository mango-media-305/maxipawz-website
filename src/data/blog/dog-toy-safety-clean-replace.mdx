A dog toy should not stay in the toy basket forever just because the dog still wants it. The useful question is: **is this toy still intact, appropriately sized, cleanable, and safe for the way this particular dog is using it today?**

AAHA advises inspecting toys regularly and discarding items damaged enough to create a choking or swallowing hazard. ASPCA guidance makes the same point from the chewing side: toys or chews that fragment, become too small, or can be swallowed in large pieces can create serious problems.

A good toy-safety routine therefore has three parts: choose appropriately, supervise real use, and inspect the toy again after the dog has changed it through chewing, tugging, throwing, carrying, or repeated washing.

## Start with the dog, not the toy label

A package may say "durable," "tough," or "for chewers," but those words do not tell you how your dog will interact with the item.

Watch the actual play style:

- Does your dog carry and retrieve the toy gently?
- Do they clamp down repeatedly in one spot?
- Do they peel seams, pick at labels, or pull fabric apart?
- Do they try to swallow pieces rather than spit them out?
- Do they shred rope fibers?
- Does a ball disappear far enough into the mouth to worry you?
- Does the dog guard the item or become difficult to interrupt around it?

Those observations matter more than assuming every dog of the same size uses toys the same way.

Our [dog product selection guide](/pet-guides/choosing-the-right-product) uses the same idea: fit the product to the individual dog's size, habits, and real-world behavior rather than buying only from a category label.

## Do a ten-second inspection before play

You do not need a complicated checklist every time. Hold the toy and look for obvious changes before handing it over.

Check for:

- cracks or splits in rubber or plastic;
- sharp or rough edges;
- exposed stuffing or squeakers;
- loose eyes, buttons, caps, or decorative pieces;
- rope strands that are separating significantly;
- seams that have opened;
- chunks missing from chew toys;
- deformation that makes a once-safe toy small enough to swallow;
- sticky residue, mold, odor, or contamination that cleaning may not solve.

The key comparison is not "does this look used?" Many toys look used quickly. The question is whether wear has changed the product in a way that creates a new hazard.

## Size can change as a toy wears down

A ball that was appropriately sized when new may not stay that way if pieces are chewed off. A chew may begin long enough to hold safely and later become a short piece that could be swallowed whole.

ASPCA guidance warns against products that are too small or that fragment into swallowable pieces. That is why "it was the right size when I bought it" is not enough.

During inspection, ask whether the remaining toy can become lodged in the mouth or throat or swallowed whole. If the answer is uncertain, replacing the item is usually a better tradeoff than trying to squeeze out a few more play sessions.

## Know the difference between cosmetic wear and structural wear

A faded color or tooth marks do not automatically mean a toy is unsafe. Structural changes matter more.

For a rubber toy, superficial scratches may be normal while a deep split that opens under pressure is a different problem. For a plush toy, matted fabric is cosmetic; a seam exposing stuffing or a squeaker is structural. For a rope toy, light fuzzing differs from long strands that can be pulled free and swallowed.

Use your hands as well as your eyes. Flex the toy. Tug gently on seams and attached parts. Press around cracks. If the product separates under a small inspection force, it is not becoming safer once the dog starts chewing hard.

## Cleaning only works when the toy is still cleanable

Regular cleaning can make toys more pleasant and reduce accumulated dirt, saliva, food residue, and outdoor grime. But cleaning instructions should come from the product's material and manufacturer guidance, not one universal recipe.

Before washing a toy:

- read the care instructions;
- remove food residue promptly from treat-dispensing toys;
- use a method compatible with the material;
- rinse away cleaning product completely when required;
- dry the item thoroughly before storing it;
- inspect again after washing, because heat or repeated cycles can expose new damage.

A toy with deep cracks, internal material you cannot reach, or a persistent odor after appropriate cleaning may have reached the end of its useful life.

Do not try to "sanitize" a structurally damaged toy back into safety.

## Repair is not always the same as safe reuse

Some pet owners sew plush seams or knot rope toys back together. Whether a repair is appropriate depends on what failed and whether the repair restores the item without creating a new loose part, hard edge, thread hazard, or weak point.

A decorative plush used gently under supervision may be repairable in a way that makes sense. A heavily chewed rubber toy with a deep crack, a shattered plastic component, or a chew that has become swallowable is a poor candidate for a home repair.

If you are improvising a repair that changes the product's original materials or construction, be conservative. Replacing the toy is often the simpler choice.

## Supervision should match the toy and the dog

"Supervise your dog" is easy advice to say and hard to use unless it has a practical meaning.

Useful supervision means being close enough to notice when play changes. If a dog begins tearing, swallowing, fixating on a loose piece, or destroying a toy in a new way, you can interrupt and remove the item before the situation progresses.

AAHA's current enrichment guidance recommends variety—rubber, plush, chew, and treat-dispensing toys—while also telling owners to inspect and discard damaged items that create choking or swallowing hazards. Variety works best when it is paired with observation.

Our [play and enrichment guide](/pet-guides/play-and-enrichment) covers the broader goal: toys should support useful behaviors and engagement, not simply occupy the dog at any cost.

## Be extra careful with edible chews and very hard items

Edible chews create a different question because pieces are expected to be consumed. The ASPCA recommends matching chews to the dog's chewing habits and avoiding products the dog can consume rapidly in large pieces.

Hardness matters too. A product that does not visibly break may still be inappropriate if it is excessively hard for the dog's teeth. If you are choosing a dental chew or chew-type product for oral care, our [dog dental-care guide](/pet-guides/dog-dental-care-routine) explains why chewing should supplement—not replace—appropriate dental care and veterinary guidance.

When a dog has a history of swallowing objects, dental disease, fractured teeth, gastrointestinal problems, or a prescribed diet, ask the veterinary team which chew options are appropriate before experimenting.

## Rotate toys instead of leaving everything available forever

Rotation can make a smaller toy collection more engaging and make inspection easier.

Try keeping part of the collection stored, bringing out a few appropriate choices, and inspecting each item as it moves in or out of rotation. This creates a natural moment to clean, discard, or replace products before damage becomes severe.

It can also help you notice what the dog actually prefers. A toy that looks exciting to a person but is consistently ignored may not need a permanent place in the basket.

## Retire a toy before the emergency, not after it

Replace or remove a toy when:

- it has become small enough to swallow or lodge in the mouth;
- pieces are breaking off;
- stuffing, squeakers, batteries, or internal components are accessible;
- rope fibers are separating significantly;
- hard edges or sharp points have developed;
- a crack opens under pressure;
- it cannot be cleaned adequately;
- the dog's play style has changed in a way that makes the item inappropriate.

If your dog swallows part of a toy, do not wait for an internet checklist to decide whether the object is safe. Contact your veterinarian for case-specific guidance, especially if the object is large, sharp, string-like, contains a battery, or the dog develops vomiting, abdominal pain, appetite changes, lethargy, difficulty passing stool, choking, or breathing trouble.

## The safest toy is the one you keep evaluating

Buying an appropriate toy is the first decision, not the last one.

Inspect it before play. Watch how your dog changes it. Clean it according to the material. Recheck size and structure. Remove loose parts early. And when the item crosses from "well loved" into "damaged enough to create a new risk," retire it without guilt.

Dogs do not need a toy to last forever. They need the toy to stay appropriate for as long as it is in use.
