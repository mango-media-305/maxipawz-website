A tick-prevention product does not make post-walk checks pointless, and finding a tick does not automatically mean a preventive has failed. A stronger routine uses **multiple layers: veterinarian-guided prevention, sensible habitat awareness, regular coat checks, and prompt removal of attached ticks**.

The CDC recommends checking pets daily for ticks, especially after they spend time outdoors. The Companion Animal Parasite Council also recommends consistent year-round tick control because tick activity and distribution vary by species, season, geography, travel, and even indoor infestation risk.

## Where to check your dog

Start with the places ticks can hide easily. The CDC highlights areas in and around the ears, around the eyelids, under the collar, under the front legs, between the toes, between the back legs, and around the tail.

Use your fingertips as well as your eyes. Slowly run your hands through the coat and feel for a small bump attached to the skin. Good lighting helps, especially on dark or dense coats.

Make the check part of the return-home routine after trails, tall grass, brush, wooded edges, or other tick habitat. If your dog dislikes handling, build tolerance gradually with brief, rewarded touch rather than forcing a long inspection all at once.

## What to do when you find an attached tick

Prompt removal matters. CDC guidance recommends removing ticks found on pets right away. CAPC describes using fine forceps or an appropriate tick-removal device and avoiding contact with tick contents.

If you are not confident removing an attached tick—or it is in a sensitive location such as near the eye—contact your veterinary clinic for guidance. Do not improvise with burning, harsh chemicals, or other techniques that may injure the dog.

After removal, wash your hands and clean the tool. Your veterinarian may also want information about when and where the tick was found, particularly if your dog later becomes ill.

## Prevention is still the foundation

Dogs can be exposed to several tick species, and those species do not all behave the same way. CAPC recommends year-round tick control and notes that the brown dog tick can establish populations inside homes and kennels.

The right preventive depends on the individual dog, location, travel, other animals in the household, health history, and product label. Ask your veterinarian which option fits your dog rather than borrowing another pet's medication or combining products on your own.

This is especially important in mixed dog-and-cat households: **never assume a product labeled for dogs is safe for cats**.

## Why you may still see a tick

Preventives differ in how they work. Some require a tick to attach before it is exposed to the active ingredient. Heavy environmental exposure can also result in repeated contact even when a product is being used correctly.

That is why the useful question is not simply, "Did I ever see a tick?" It is whether the preventive is being given correctly and on schedule, whether exposure is unusually heavy, and whether environmental control or a different veterinary plan is needed.

If ticks are repeatedly appearing inside the home, particularly brown dog ticks, professional pest-control support may be necessary in addition to treating pets.

## Watch the dog after a known bite

A tick bite does not mean disease is inevitable. Still, the CDC advises watching for changes in behavior or appetite after a known or suspected bite because signs of tickborne disease may appear days or weeks later.

Contact your veterinarian if your dog develops lethargy, reduced appetite, fever, lameness, unusual bruising, weakness, or other concerning changes after tick exposure. Symptoms overlap with many conditions, so home diagnosis based on a tick photo is not reliable.

## Make outdoor safety a system

Keep prevention dates in one place. Check your dog's coat after higher-risk outings. Inspect collars and harnesses while your hands are already on the dog. Keep your veterinary contact information accessible in your [dog first aid kit](/pet-guides/dog-first-aid-kit), and use our [collar and accessory guide](/pet-guides/collars-and-accessories) to keep walking gear secure and easy to inspect.

Tick safety works best as a routine, not a reaction after the first problem appears.
