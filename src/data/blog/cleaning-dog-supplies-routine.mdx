Dog supplies collect saliva, food residue, hair, dirt and whatever comes in from daily life. A simple cleaning routine is more useful than waiting until an item looks obviously dirty—and it does not require disinfecting everything every day.

## Separate cleaning from disinfecting

CDC explains that cleaning removes dirt and germs with soap or detergent, while disinfecting uses chemicals to kill germs on surfaces. For most routine pet items, cleaning is usually sufficient. Disinfection becomes more important in certain situations, including illness or contamination with urine or feces.

Always follow the item's care label first. A dishwasher-safe bowl, machine-washable bed and rubber toy may need completely different methods.

## Give bowls the most frequent attention

CDC's example schedule recommends cleaning wet-food bowls after every use and dry-food and water bowls daily. Remove leftover food first, wash thoroughly, rinse away detergent and dry appropriately.

Wash your hands after handling pet food, bowls and supplies. If a bowl is cracked, deeply scratched or otherwise difficult to clean, replacement may be more practical than trying to sanitize a damaged surface indefinitely.

## Build toys into the calendar

CDC gives monthly cleaning as an example for pet toys, with more frequent cleaning when items are visibly dirty or smell bad. The right method depends on the material. Hard dishwasher-safe toys may tolerate a dishwasher; some soft toys can go through a washing machine and dryer.

Inspect while you clean. Loose pieces, torn seams or damaged rubber are product-safety problems, not hygiene problems. Our [toy safety guide](/pet-guides/dog-toy-safety-clean-replace) explains when replacement is the safer choice.

## Wash bedding on a predictable cycle

CDC's example schedule suggests weekly cleaning for beds and blankets. Follow the fabric label, remove excess hair and make sure bedding dries fully before returning it to your dog's resting area.

If your dog has sensitive skin, ask your veterinarian whether a fragrance-free detergent or another change makes sense for that individual dog.

## Clean more often when circumstances change

CDC recommends more frequent cleaning and disinfection when a pet is sick or when someone in the household is at higher risk of severe illness from germs. Supplies contaminated by urine or feces deserve additional attention.

Use disinfectants exactly as their labels direct, keep pets away while required and store chemicals securely. Never mix cleaning chemicals.

A good routine is easy to repeat: bowls daily or after use as appropriate, bedding weekly, toys on a regular cycle, and immediate cleaning when something is soiled. Pair hygiene with inspection and you can catch both dirt and product damage before either becomes a bigger problem.
